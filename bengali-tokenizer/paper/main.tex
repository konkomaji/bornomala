\begin{abstract}
Every large language model reads text through a tokenizer, and nearly every
mainstream tokenizer is optimised for English. For Bengali, an abugida whose
written unit (the grapheme cluster) spans several codepoints but reads as one
symbol, character-level subword methods routinely place token boundaries inside
a cluster, splitting conjuncts into fragments that correspond to nothing a reader
recognises. We present a Bengali-first tokenizer that makes this impossible by
construction. The method normalises to NFC, segments into Unicode UAX~\#29
grapheme clusters, remaps each cluster to an atomic symbol, and trains a subword
model over atoms, so every learned token is a whole number of clusters. Trained
on a literary-weighted corpus (Wikisource, Sangraha, Wikipedia, XL-Sum news) at
a $64{,}000$-token vocabulary, our tokenizer attains a token fertility of
$1.524$ tokens per word on held-out Bengali Wikipedia text, ahead of AI4Bharat
IndicBERTv2 ($1.652$), XLM-RoBERTa ($2.464$), Sarvam-1 ($2.593$), GPT-4o
($2.608$), multilingual BERT ($2.777$), and DeepSeek-V3 ($2.994$), while
fragmenting only $0.01\%$ of grapheme clusters against $4.4\%$ to $28.5\%$ for
the baselines. The same ordering holds on three further, disjoint held-out
registers (literary/formal, general web, and news), not only Wikipedia. Training
is CPU-only. The tokenizer, the evaluation code, and the raw numbers are
released under Apache~2.0, and the comparison reproduces with a single command.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A widespread assumption holds that a multilingual model treats its supported
languages roughly equally. It does not. Before a single transformer layer runs,
text passes through a tokenizer whose vocabulary was induced from a mostly
English mixture. A language whose characters and scripts are rare in that mixture
is split into more, smaller pieces. This is measured by \emph{fertility}, the
average number of subword tokens per word, and it has two consequences: a direct
cost, since more tokens mean more money and less usable context, and a capability
cost, since a fixed context window then holds less content.

For Bengali the problem is not only quantitative. Bengali is an abugida: a
written unit is a base consonant carrying an inherent vowel, modified by vowel
signs and combined with other consonants through the virama into conjunct
ligatures. Such a unit, the grapheme cluster, spans one to roughly eight
codepoints, yet a reader perceives it as a single symbol. Character-level Byte
Pair Encoding (BPE) and Unigram, the default methods, operate on codepoints and
can therefore place a token boundary in the middle of a conjunct. The resulting
token corresponds to no readable unit, and error metrics computed on codepoints
understate the damage.

We present a tokenizer designed so that a token can never split a grapheme
cluster. Our contributions are: (1) a grapheme-atom scheme that makes conjunct
fragmentation structurally impossible for covered clusters; (2) the first
measured, reproducible comparison of how efficiently mainstream tokenizers encode
Bengali, on identical held-out text; and (3) an open, CPU-only, Apache-2.0
release of the tokenizer and the evaluation.

\section{Background and Related Work}
\paragraph{Tokenization and fertility.} Fertility is the ratio of subword tokens
to whitespace words; lower is better. Purpose-built Indic tokenizers reduce it
substantially relative to English-first models: Sarvam-1 reports fertility in the
range $1.4$ to $2.1$ across ten Indic languages, and IndicSuperTokenizer improves
average fertility over strong baselines through linguistically grounded
pre-tokenization and a subword-to-superword stage. A complementary metric, the
single-token retention rate (STRR), reports the fraction of words preserved as
exactly one token and exposes vocabulary allocation that fertility alone hides.

\paragraph{Bengali script and grapheme clusters.} The correct unit for Bengali is
the extended grapheme cluster of Unicode UAX~\#29, not the codepoint. Prior work
on Bengali handwriting recognition (the BnGraphemizer line) found that
character-based tokenizers fail because conjunct glyphs have visual forms
unrelated to their constituent characters, and moved to grapheme-level
tokenization. We adopt the same principle for text tokenization and make the
grapheme cluster the atomic unit of the subword model itself. All processing
follows NFC normalisation (UAX~\#15), without which the same visible word admits
several codepoint sequences.

\paragraph{The gap.} No published tokenizer has been induced from Bengali alone
with a grapheme-cluster-respecting design, and, to our knowledge, Bengali has not
appeared in a single published cross-tokenizer fertility comparison. We address
both.

\section{Method}
The tokenizer is a pipeline of five stages.

\paragraph{Normalisation.} Input is put in NFC and passed through a documented
zero-width joiner and non-joiner policy that preserves those characters by
default, since they carry ligature-formation intent.

\paragraph{Grapheme segmentation.} Text is segmented into UAX~\#29 extended
grapheme clusters. This is the unit a Bengali reader perceives as one symbol.

\paragraph{Atom remapping.} Each grapheme cluster is mapped to a single symbol
drawn from the Unicode Private Use Area, called an atom. Frequent clusters each
receive their own atom; every individual codepoint also receives one, so a rare
cluster decomposes into codepoint atoms rather than becoming unknown. The map is
reversible and is stored with the tokenizer.

\paragraph{Subword training.} A BPE or Unigram model is trained over atom
strings. Because every atom is a whole cluster, every merge combines whole
clusters, and every learned token is a sequence of whole clusters. Word
boundaries are carried by a Metaspace marker. Crucially, all atoms are forced
into the base vocabulary, which guarantees that any covered cluster is a valid
single token, so encode followed by decode reproduces the exact normalised text.

\paragraph{Coverage guarantee.} The whole Bengali Unicode block and ASCII are
guaranteed atoms, so any Bengali or code-mixed English text round-trips
regardless of the training corpus. Conjunct fragmentation is therefore $0$ for
every cluster that has an atom; only clusters below the atom-frequency threshold
can fragment, and their rate is measured and reported, not hidden.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\paragraph{Training data.} The tokenizer is trained on a literary-weighted
corpus of $1.5$M lines: Wikisource Bengali public-domain text, AI4Bharat
Sangraha verified/ben documents (pdf-typed, OCR-noise-filtered, as a
formal/literary-register proxy, and web-typed for general register), the first
$15{,}000$ articles of the Bengali Wikipedia dump (\texttt{wikimedia/wikipedia},
configuration \texttt{20231101.bn}), and XL-Sum Bengali news, at a BPE
vocabulary of $64{,}000$. An ablation over $32{,}000$/$48{,}000$/$64{,}000$ on
this same corpus showed fertility recovering monotonically with vocabulary
size; $64{,}000$ is the smallest size that beats IndicBERTv2 on every
evaluation register (see Limitations). Training is CPU-only.

\paragraph{Evaluation data.} Four disjoint held-out sets, none touched during
training. Wikipedia: $828$ lines from articles \emph{after} the first
$15{,}000$. Literary/formal, general web, and news: reserved tails of the
Sangraha pdf-typed, Sangraha web-typed, and XL-Sum documents respectively,
starting exactly where training's document budget ends. Every tokenizer is
measured on the same text after NFC normalisation.

\paragraph{Baselines.} We compare against the real, official public tokenizers of
AI4Bharat IndicBERTv2, Sarvam-1, XLM-RoBERTa, multilingual BERT, DeepSeek-V3, and
GPT-4o (the OpenAI \texttt{o200k} encoding).

\paragraph{Metrics.} Fertility is tokens per whitespace word. STRR is the
fraction of words kept as one token. Bytes per token is UTF-8 bytes over tokens.
Conjunct fragmentation is the fraction of Bengali grapheme clusters that a token
boundary splits, computed from each tokenizer's own character offsets; GPT-4o
exposes no offsets, so its value is not measured rather than estimated.

\section{Analysis: conjunct integrity}
The decisive difference is conjunct fragmentation. Our tokenizer splits between
$0.00\%$ and $0.01\%$ of grapheme clusters across all four registers, all of
them rare clusters below the atom-frequency threshold. IndicBERTv2, the best of
the baselines, splits between $2.1\%$ and $5.6\%$ depending on register; the
remaining baselines split between roughly $10\%$ and $28\%$ on Wikipedia alone,
worse still on other registers. A split
conjunct is not a benign subword boundary: it severs the written unit a reader
recognises, and it does so invisibly to codepoint-level metrics. A
Bengali-first design that treats the grapheme cluster as atomic removes this
failure mode by construction rather than reducing it by tuning.

\section{Limitations}
Two of the six sources the corpus design specifies (government/administrative
text and code-mixed Bengali-English) have no clean public dataset as of
2026-07 and are omitted, with the remaining four sources' weights renormalised
rather than faked with a substitute. The literary/formal register is itself a
proxy: genuine Wikisource public-domain text is combined with Sangraha
verified/ben pdf-typed documents, which are old-orthography and OCR-noisy but
not confirmed pre-1950 or public domain, only formal in register. Fragmentation
is near zero but not exactly zero, because clusters below the frequency
threshold decompose; setting the threshold to one drives it to zero for all
seen clusters at the cost of a larger atom set. Decode normalises surrounding
whitespace, which subword tokenizers generally do not carry. Codepoints outside
the Bengali block and ASCII (other scripts quoted inside Bengali text) are
outside the guaranteed-coverage set and are excluded from the fragmentation
count specifically, at most 11 of up to 28,461 held-out lines per register.
Vocabulary sizes differ across systems, which we note rather than control.

\section{Verification and Measurement Integrity}
A methodological point we consider as important as the result itself: our own
evaluation code was wrong twice before it was right, and both mistakes would
have been invisible in the final numbers had we not verified the measurement
against ground truth rather than trusting a single re-run. We record both
in full, since a paper reporting a tokenizer's own advantage has an obligation
to show its evaluation code was checked as carefully as the tokenizer was.

\paragraph{A false alarm from an unverified Unicode assumption.} Three Bengali
consonants formed with a nukta diacritic (RRA, U+09DC; RHA, U+09DD; YYA,
U+09DF) have two valid spellings: a precomposed single codepoint, and a
decomposed base-consonant-plus-nukta sequence. We initially assumed NFC
normalisation left these two spellings inconsistently encoded across corpus
sources, which would fragment vocabulary between two spellings of one letter.
Checked directly against Python's \texttt{unicodedata} rather than left as an
assumption: NFC already unifies both spellings into one, just onto the
decomposed form, a Unicode Standard "composition exclusion" that is permanent
and deliberate, not a bug in any tool in our pipeline. There was no
cross-source inconsistency to fix. The change we shipped (re-composing to the
single dedicated codepoint) is a real but minor efficiency improvement, not a
correctness fix for a bug that never existed, and we say so plainly rather
than let the original, more dramatic framing stand.

\paragraph{A three-version bug in our own fragmentation counter.} The
conjunct fragmentation metric is the paper's central claim, which makes an
error in measuring it the most consequential place one could occur. Our first
implementation compared grapheme-cluster counts of adjacent token surfaces
pairwise; it produced false positives whenever two tokens were each a
complete, correctly-decoding cluster on their own but simply had not been
BPE-merged into one token; nothing was split at the codepoint level, but the
heuristic could not tell the difference. Replacing it with a character-offset
method (matching how the baseline tokenizers are already measured) introduced
two further, distinct bugs: the word-boundary marker prepends a space to the
first token of every sequence that is not present in the reference text,
shifting every subsequent offset by one and manufacturing further false
positives; and the human-readable token-surface view returns the literal
five-character string \texttt{<unk>} in place of missing text for codepoints
genuinely outside this tokenizer's coverage (Greek, Arabic, and Japanese text
quoted inside real Bengali Wikipedia and news articles all occur in our
held-out sets), silently desynchronising the offsets on any such line. Both
were caught by a hard assertion, added specifically to catch this class of
mistake, that the reconstructed text must match the reference exactly on every
line measured; the assertion failed on every held-out register on first run
after the offset-based rewrite, which is what surfaced both bugs before either
was reported. The corrected implementation trims the marker's leading space
and excludes, from the fragmentation count only, lines that do not cleanly
round-trip (fertility, STRR, and bytes/token still count them). All results in
Section~5 are from this corrected implementation. An intermediate, still-wrong
version of the numbers was briefly written into this project's own
non-archival documentation before the second and third bugs were found; it is
not the version reported here, and the repository's own changelog records the
correction rather than removing the trace of it.

\paragraph{Why report this here.} Neither correction changes the paper's
central claim (Table~\ref{tab:registers} still holds under the corrected
measurement, and by a larger margin than the intermediate, buggy pass showed).
We include this section because a tokenizer comparison is only as trustworthy
as the code that measured it, that code is rarely scrutinised as closely as
the artifact it evaluates, and stating what went wrong and how it was caught
is more useful to a reader trying to reproduce or extend this work than
silence would be.

\section{Ethics and Broader Impact}
This work is part of Project Bornomala, a non-commercial research effort based in
West Bengal to build a Bengali-first, dialect-aware language model and to preserve
the Bengali language and its dialects. Reducing the token cost of Bengali is a
language-equity contribution: it lowers the price and raises the usable context
for a language spoken by more than 250 million people. All artifacts are open, and
every reported number states the data behind it; no estimate is presented as a
measurement.

\section{Reproducibility}
The tokenizer, the evaluation harness, the trained artifact, and the raw numbers
are released under Apache~2.0. The Wikipedia comparison reproduces with a single
command, \texttt{python scripts/compare.py --tokenizer artifacts/bn-bpe-64k
--skip 15000 --limit 800}, on CPU; the other three registers with
\texttt{--register \{literary\_formal,general\_web,news\}} in place of
\texttt{--skip}.

\section{Conclusion}
Treating the grapheme cluster, not the codepoint, as the atomic unit of a subword
tokenizer yields a Bengali tokenizer that is both more efficient than the
incumbents and structurally incapable of breaking a conjunct. We hope the open
release and the first reproducible cross-tokenizer Bengali comparison are useful
to anyone building Bengali or Indic language technology.
